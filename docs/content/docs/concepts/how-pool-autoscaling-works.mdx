title: "How pool autoscaling works"
description: "How a sandbox pool sizes itself to claim demand, and what the minimum, initial, and maximum pool sizes actually control."

A sandbox pool keeps sandboxes booted before anyone asks for them. When a client
claims from the pool, it attaches to a machine that is already running, so the
claim takes seconds instead of a full boot. The question a pool must answer
continuously is how many sandboxes to keep alive: too few and claims wait for
boots, too many and idle machines burn capacity.

A pool answers that question in one of two ways, and every pool uses exactly
one. A *static* pool holds a fixed replica target that changes only when you
change it. An *autoscaled* pool derives its target from claim demand and keeps
re-deriving it as demand moves.

## Claims are the demand signal

An autoscaled pool watches one number: the count of live claims against it,
counting both claims already bound to a sandbox and claims still waiting for
one. The desired pool size is that count, clamped between the pool's minimum
and maximum. Ten live claims ask for a pool of ten. When they release, the ask
falls back toward the minimum.

Two refinements keep this signal honest. The pool acts on its recent peak
rather than the instantaneous value, so a brief dip between two bursts does not
tear capacity down and rebuild it. And claims are counted even when they are
created and released quickly, so short-lived sessions still register as demand
instead of slipping between measurements.

A claim that arrives while no sandbox is ready does not fail immediately. It
waits, and the waiting claim is itself the demand that raises the target and
provisions the sandbox it is waiting for. The claim keeps trying to bind until
its bind deadline, five minutes by default and configurable per claim, and only
fails when the deadline passes.

## A claimed sandbox still counts

The least obvious property of the model is that a claimed sandbox continues to
occupy a slot in the pool's accounting. Binding a claim removes the sandbox
from the warm set, but not from the pool's size. Claiming therefore does not
directly spawn a replacement. The replacement appears because the new claim
raised demand by one, which raised the target by one, and the pool tops up to
the target.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: a pool whose target equals its live
claims holds zero warm spares. Warm spares exist only where the target exceeds
current demand, which is exactly what the minimum pool size buys. Autoscaling
reacts within seconds, but a fresh sandbox still takes a boot to become ready,
and no reactive policy can conjure a booted machine inside a claim's bind
window. When the latency of the next claim matters, a nonzero minimum is the
only guarantee.

## How capacity returns

A pooled sandbox is born booting, becomes ready, and waits to be claimed. What
happens after release depends on the claim's shutdown policy. With the default
`Retain` policy the sandbox restarts in place to a clean state and rejoins the
warm set, which is far cheaper than booting new capacity, so a busy pool mostly
recycles the same machines. With the `Delete` policy the sandbox is destroyed,
and the pool boots a fresh replacement only if the target still calls for one.

## How the pool shrinks

Scale-down is deliberately slower than scale-up. Because the pool acts on its
held peak, capacity drains roughly five minutes after demand falls, not the
instant a claim releases.

Only unclaimed sandboxes are candidates for removal. A claimed sandbox left the
warm set when it was bound, so shrinking can never touch a machine someone is
using. Among the candidates, sandboxes that are still booting are removed
before ready ones, so a shrinking pool sheds the capacity that was least useful
first. Each removal also checks that a claim did not grab the sandbox at the
last moment; if one did, the sandbox survives and the pool re-evaluates on the
next cycle.

A pool with a minimum of zero drains completely once its last claim clears and
the held peak expires. That means "nothing idle", not "stranded at zero": the
next claim reactivates the pool, at the cost of waiting for the first boot.

## What the three parameters control

The autoscaling configuration is three numbers. The *minimum pool size*
(default 0) is the durable floor and the only setting that guarantees warm
spares; raising it trades idle capacity for instant claims. The *initial pool
size* (default 0) seeds the pool once at creation so its first users do not
wait for the first scale-up to react; it is not a floor, and after creation the
target belongs to demand and decays toward the minimum. The *maximum pool size*
(default 50) is the hard ceiling on capacity and cost; demand beyond it waits
as pending claims until capacity frees up or bind deadlines expire.
